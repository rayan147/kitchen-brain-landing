WHAT GOES WRONG

The spreadsheet works until the job changes.

Most kitchens cost on a spreadsheet, and it holds until something moves. A supplier price changes. The client adds forty guests. A case arrives short. Each one is a number somebody has to find and change by hand.

So every job starts as the last job’s sheet, saved under a new name. That is the right move with the tools most kitchens have. It is also how the price you quote and the price you pay drift apart without anybody getting anything wrong.

Those four are my own week, not a survey, and the last one is the one I built this for.

01

You quote from an old price

The vendor moved the case price and you changed it where you saw it. The sheet you quoted this job from is a copy of last month’s, and it still holds the old one.

02

You rebuild the same order four times

Forty more guests, and the same number has to change in the recipe math, the shopping list, the prep plan and the pack check. Four edits, and the one you miss turns up at the dock.

03

You retype the list to buy it

You copy the rows out by hand: one message per supplier, then a checklist at the dock, then whatever the truck was short, typed back in.

04

You learn the margin after service

The invoices arrive after the event and go in a folder, not in the sheet you quoted from. Weeks later, if you go and check, they decide whether the price worked.

AND THE QUIET ONE

Those four are the slow ones. The one that costs money is quieter. A sheet totals what is in it, so a missing fact does not stop the number. It shrinks it.

A PRICE YOU HAVE NOT GOT YET

The beef is blank while you wait on the invoice. The plate cost still prints, short by the beef and looking just as confident as a right answer.

A CONVERSION NOBODY CHECKED

The recipe is in grams, the pack is in pounds, and whoever typed the number between them is not in the kitchen today.

Neither is slower. They are wrong, and the screen does not say so. CostCook stops instead. A missing price or a missing conversion holds the costing and names the line it is waiting on.

TRIM AND YIELD

The case has the skins on it. Your recipe does not.

The recipe uses 60 grams of cored tomato. The case price still has to pay for the part trimmed away. Follow that one line from the quantity in the dish to the cost on the recipe.

Recorded purchase
25 lb case · $29.14 · $2.57/kg as purchased

Sixty grams used in the recipe, divided by a trim yield of zero point nine one, equals sixty-six grams required to buy. Sixty-six grams at two dollars and fifty-seven cents per kilogram equals a seventeen-cent line cost.

What it is not.
The estimate is not a retail-label compliance claim, and the printed sheet says so.
CostCook detects, it never certifies. Halal and kosher can only ever come back as a check. Slaughter and certification are facts the app cannot see.
